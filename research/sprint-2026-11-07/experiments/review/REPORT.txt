C. WHAT THE CONCLUSIONS MISS

- Basin size.  The only repeats are on plateaus of 0.8-3.4 M states, none on 3^7 ones.  Landing
  probability scales with plateau size, so the "diagonal discovery curve" is a property of the
  small-plateau majority, and arm C (which lands on big coupled products) may be finding FEWER
  distinct cores per worker-hour than A, not more.  A capture-recapture estimate of the number of
  88 cores, weighted by size, is the right instrument; it needs the cheap identity over all
  1,869 sampled states, and that job (sig.py) is not running.
- The plateau graph.  Half of the hops along a chain change more than 30 % of the values and
  another fifth are 2-value swaps: the 88 level is a connected graph of cores under the +1 band,
  and whether an 87 is reachable is a property of that graph (which cores have down-exits, how
  far they are from where descents land), not of the number of nodes.  The sprint has the data
  to draw it (transitions with Jaccard and size) and has not.
- Paired design.  A, B and C share their first six roots.  Per-root comparisons of time to 88,
  landing plateau and depth were available for free and would have removed root variance from
  F6 and F8; later restarts use different roots, so this holds only for the first chain.
- Noise floor.  Run-to-run spread is about 3 gates on 64x64 maps and 1 gate on 4x4 maps at
  NIST's depth, so "predictions" of 2-3 gates (rules for 4x4 maps) and "gains" of 1 gate are
  not distinguishable from repeats of the same search.
- Depth.  Neither the hunt (depth-5 88s in 2 + 7 + 0 chains) nor the bench (no 42@5 reproduced)
  reaches the shallow records; depth_window=none makes this worse.  For the record table, which is
  per depth, the established bench effect may be a loss, and no capped test exists.
